# The 8 Principles of AI in PPPM

Source: PMI, *The Standard for Artificial Intelligence in Portfolio, Program, and Project Management* (June 2026), Chapter 2. Listed without weighting or order — none is more important than the others, and a strong plan should be able to speak to all eight, not just the ones that are easy or flattering.

Use this file when you need to check an AI initiative, pitch, or plan against the full set, or when you need the PMI-correct name and framing for a specific angle someone raised.

## 1. Strategic Value — "the value-creation anchor"
AI is a means to organizational value, not an end in itself. Every AI initiative — no matter how small — should trace back to a specific business goal, and that goal should be revisited continuously, not just at kickoff, because the technology and the environment both keep moving. If someone can't say what business outcome an AI initiative serves, that's the first gap to close, before any conversation about tooling or governance.

## 2. Risk — "evolving risk adaptation"
AI introduces risk categories that traditional PPPM risk registers don't cover well: algorithmic bias, data privacy exposure, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, and reputational risk from AI decisions that turn out to be wrong or unexplainable. The standard anchors risk management in Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) oversight plus technical controls (data masking, encryption, access control, audit logs). Because AI risk evolves as models and use cases change, this principle explicitly calls for continuous monitoring and adaptation — not a one-time risk assessment at project start.

## 3. Governance and Compliance — "responsible AI management"
Clear governance structures — defined roles, decision authority, procedures, and standards — are what make "responsible AI use" concrete rather than aspirational. This principle calls for AI governance to be integrated into the organization's existing governance, risk, and compliance (GRC) structures rather than run as a parallel, bolted-on process, and for that governance to keep evolving as AI technology does.

## 4. People and Culture — "innovation fosters empowerment"
Successful AI adoption depends on the people side as much as the technical side: building AI literacy, addressing resistance to change directly rather than around it, and creating psychological safety so people can experiment, raise concerns, and learn without fear of blame. Leaders are called out specifically as responsible for reinforcing strategic goals and supporting skill development — this isn't treated as an HR-only concern.

## 5. Ethics and Professional Responsibility — "ethical AI stewardship"
This is about the practitioner's own conduct, not just the system's design. PPPM professionals are expected to maintain an ethics framework across the entire AI life cycle, prioritize transparency, explainability, auditability, and accountability, and ensure AI adoption serves stakeholders' best interests — not just the interests of whoever is sponsoring the initiative. See `ethics-and-hitl.md` for the fuller list of specific ethical challenges this principle is meant to guard against.

## 6. Stakeholder Engagement — "connect, align, and deliver"
AI initiatives fail when stakeholders are informed rather than engaged. This principle is closely linked to the Managing Stakeholder Expectations About AI performance domain (see `performance-domains.md`) — change management, communication, and training aren't separate workstreams from the technical rollout, they're part of how the initiative succeeds.

## 7. Optimization and Innovation — "evolving through innovation"
AI initiatives should be built to keep improving, not to be delivered once and left alone. This principle is the standard's clearest statement that a "ship it and move on" mentality is a mismatch for AI work — continuous improvement is treated as inherent to the discipline, not a nice-to-have.

## 8. Data Quality — "input for impact"
The standard treats data quality as foundational rather than a downstream technical concern: AI is only as good as the data feeding it, and data governance and quality assurance should be treated as core PPPM activities, not something delegated entirely to a data team.

## How to use this in a conversation
When someone challenges an AI plan, it's usually because one of these eight is under-addressed, not because the whole plan is wrong. Naming the specific principle in play ("this is really a Governance and Compliance gap, not a Strategic Value gap") is more persuasive and more precise than generic pushback, and it signals fluency with the actual standard rather than intuition.
